Example 1: Peeling an apple
I have peeled an apple so many times that the memory is no longer of any single apple, any single knife, any single hand. What remains is a sequence of physical events.
First, the cold weight in my palm. The skin feels slightly cool and faintly damp. My thumb rests on one side, fingers curled around the other. The surface has a small amount of friction—enough that the apple does not slip when I press gently.
The blade touches the skin just below the stem. I tilt the knife so the flat side angles upward, perhaps ten or fifteen degrees. The edge slides underneath the skin. A thin strip of peel lifts away from the flesh. The strip curls as it comes off, forming a loose spiral. I hear a soft, dry scraping sound, followed by a wetter sound as juice seeps out of the exposed flesh.
I rotate the apple slowly with my thumb and middle finger. The blade continues to cut a shallow channel. The peel runs in a continuous band around the apple. The band narrows and widens slightly depending on how steady my hand is. When the blade cuts too deep, a thicker piece of flesh comes off with the peel, white and wet. When it cuts too shallow, the peel tears, and I see the pale pith underneath.
The apple grows slicker in my hand as more juice coats my skin. The exposed flesh starts as cream-colored, then darkens slightly within seconds. The core becomes visible as a faint five-pointed shape through the thin layer of flesh remaining over it.
The removed peel piles up on the cutting board. It forms a coiled heap. The outer side of each curl is red or green or yellow, depending on the apple. The inner side is pale, almost white. Light passes through the thinnest parts of the peel.
When the blade reaches the bottom of the apple, the last piece of peel detaches. The apple now has no skin. Its entire surface is wet, smooth, and slippery. Small droplets of juice bead up on the flesh. The stem is gone. The calyx at the bottom is empty.
I do not eat it. I hold the naked apple in my palm. My fingers leave faint prints on the wet surface. The peel pile sits to one side. The knife has a thin film of juice along the blade.
That is the memory: the weight, the rotation, the cutting, the wetness, the pile. Nothing more.

Example 2: Rinsing a soapy mug
The dishwater stands cool and grey in the sink, a thin raft of bubbles idling against the stainless‑steel rim. The mug sits beside the drying rack, its outer surface filmed with a slippery lather that has lost all heat. A fork rests on the drainer, still wet, and a plate leans against the wall, pale circles of dried soap marring its rim. My right hand is pruney and cold from ten minutes of scrubbing.
I reach for the faucet lever – a single, arched arm of chrome that juts over the basin. My palm wraps the stubby cylinder; the metal is slick with condensation and gives a faint, gritty resistance before the valve yields. Water erupts from the spout in a storm of white noise. The stream is clear at first, then cools to a steady, glass‑rod column that punches into the sink.
I lift the mug with my left hand. Its handle is still sudsy, and my fingers slip a little before I adjust my grip – thumb inside, three fingers curled around the outside. The ceramic bears the faint, residual warmth of the original dishwater, now fading. I tilt the mug mouth‑down into the stream.
The water hits the interior curve with a hollow, drumming sound that flattens as the mug fills. Swirls of soap cloud the water immediately, a milky nebula blooming and spilling over the rim. I rotate the mug slowly, letting the jet chase the film. The outer wall passes under the stream; the lather thins to a transparent sheen, then vanishes, revealing the deep forest‑green glaze beneath. Droplets bead on the surface, catching the kitchen's overhead light.
I shift to a two‑handed grip – right hand cupping the base, left still at the handle – and roll the mug in a gentle arc so the water reaches every quadrant. The last stubborn patch of foam, a crescent clinging to the inside bottom, dissolves after three revolutions. I hold the mug up to the light, checking for any glint of residue. The water runs off in sheets, leaving a wet, uniform gloss.
Satisfied, I close the faucet with a sharp downward press. The noise stops, leaving only the drip‑drip from the spout and a thin, clear puddle spiralling toward the drain. I give the mug a final, vigorous shake over the sink, sending a fine spray against the basin walls. Then I set it, still beaded with cool water, on the drying rack between a glass and the fork. My hands go still; the only sound is the slow tick of droplets hitting the steel.
That is the memory: the cold lever, the sudden rush, the turning green ceramic, the quiet.

Example 3: Filling a kettle
I have filled the kettle so many mornings that the memory is no longer of any single kettle, any single faucet, any single kitchen. What remains is a sequence of physical events.
First, the kettle sits on the counter, its belly dark and cool, droplets of last night's condensation beading along its brushed‑steel curve. My right hand reaches for the handle—curved black plastic, slightly textured where the thumb rests, still faintly slick from yesterday's water. The kettle rocks forward off its base, a low ceramic ring scraping dry against the counter, and then lifts. The weight is hollow, unfulfilled, the empty vessel registering as a promise.
I walk three steps to the sink. The faucet neck is arched above a basin lined with a few stray drops, morning light glinting off the chrome. With my left hand I push the kettle under the spout until the opening is centered, the rim nearly touching the aerator. The cold porcelain of the sink edge presses against my knuckles.
My right hand finds the lever—a single smooth arc of polished nickel. I push it back until resistance gives and water rushes out in a thick, aerated column. The sound shifts instantly from a hollow spray against the sink to a deeper, drumming note as the steam hits the kettle's bare metal interior. A fine mist splashes upward, cooling the air near my face. The water level climbs fast: first a shallow pool shimmering at the bottom, then a deeper, swirling body whose surface shatters into concentric ripples under the continuing flow. The kettle's mass grows steadily, the handle pressing more insistently into my grip, the metal skin transferring the cold of the incoming water to my palm.
I watch the waterline approach the little stamped line three‑quarters of the way up—the "max" mark, etched in faint grey. I nudge the lever back toward center. The stream thins, its roar softening, and I cut it off completely. A few last drops detach from the aerator and fall silently into the now‑still water. The kettle is full, its weight a solid downward pull in my hand, no longer hollow. I lift it away, careful not to spill, and a thin trickle runs from the pour spout back into the sink.
Walking back, the kettle's base meets the counter with a soft, muffled clink, the heating element contact kissing the metal. The water inside retains the chill of the pipes, but soon it will heat. The morning ritual is nearly complete.

Example 4: Pouring water from a pitcher into a glass
I have poured water from a pitcher into a glass so many times that the memory is no longer of any single pitcher, any particular glass, any one table. What remains is a sequence of physical events.
First, the pitcher is on the table to my right. It is a heavy glass or clear plastic container, wider at the bottom, narrow at the neck, with a handle on the side opposite the spout. The surface is cool and smooth, faintly beaded with condensation. The drinking glass stands a few inches away, upright, empty, its rim dry and clean.
My right hand reaches out, fingers open. The thumb curls around the top of the handle, the three middle fingers slide inside the handle's loop, and the palm presses against the cold curved surface. I lift. The weight shifts—there is a moment of resistance as the water's inertia holds, then the pitcher rises smoothly. The elbow flexes, the wrist tilts slightly to keep the spout level. The pitcher moves through the air in a short arc, crossing the distance to the glass.
The spout hovers an inch above the glass's rim, centered. I begin to rotate the pitcher at the wrist. The angle of the body changes from nearly vertical to about forty degrees. Water moves inside; I hear a low gurgle, then the first transparent thread spills from the spout. It catches the light—a brief glint—and falls. The stream is round, coherent, a liquid column that widens imperceptibly as it descends.
The water strikes the bottom of the glass with a soft, splashing hiss. It pools there, spreading outward to the walls, climbing them in a smooth, steady advance. The pitch of the sound rises as the cavity shrinks. Bubbles, tiny and silver, rise through the body of the water and pop at the surface.
I feel the pitcher's weight diminish by small increments, the balance shifting as the water volume decreases. My hand adjusts the tilt angle continuously, keeping the flow smooth and the stream from striking the rim. The glass grows heavier in the periphery of my attention, though my left hand is not touching it.
The water level approaches the desired point—three-quarters full. I start to reverse the wrist rotation, slowing the pour. The stream constricts, becomes a thin ribbon, then a series of distinct drops that stretch and snap back. A final drop clings to the spout's lip, wavers, falls. The surface of the water in the glass quivers and settles into a flat, clear disc. There is a faint ring of moisture where the stream entered; it quickly merges into the still body.
I lower the pitcher back to the table with a controlled descent, the bottom finding the surface with a soft thud. My fingers release the handle, and I feel the residual coolness on my palm. The pitcher is noticeably lighter now, the remaining water sloshing gently. The glass stands filled, its contents motionless, the room's light bending through the water and casting a faint, wavering shadow on the table.
That is the memory: the reach, the lift, the pour, the sound, the light, the weight, the stillness.

Example 5: Watering plants
I have watered the houseplants so many times that the memory is no longer of any single plant, any single watering can. What remains is a sequence of physical events.
First, the heft of the watering can in my right hand. It's full—cold water sloshing against galvanised metal, the weight pulling at my wrist. The handle is smooth, slightly chilled, and my fingers curl around it with a firm but relaxed grip. My left hand steadies the base, fingers spread across the curved metal belly. I can feel the water's inertia as I lift it from the floor—a small moment of resistance, then the whole thing rises. The can holds roughly six litres; its mass, when full, is about six kilograms. The spout is a long, narrow tube of the same dull silver, tapering to a rose—a perforated cap that will break the stream into gentle droplets.
I walk three steps to the first plant, a broad-leafed philodendron in a terracotta pot. The pot rests on a saucer on a wooden shelf. The soil surface is dry and crusted, a shade lighter than the rich dark brown it shows when damp. Small fissures radiate from the stem base. The leaves, deep green and glossy, droop ever so slightly—a barely perceptible loss of turgor.
I position the watering can spout about ten centimetres above the soil, tilted downward at perhaps twenty degrees. My left hand releases the can and moves to steady the pot rim, thumb on the inside, fingers on the outside. I begin to pour. The water inside the can rushes forward with a soft gurgle; the weight shifts toward the front of my hand. The first droplets fall from the rose in a wide, even spray, pattering against the dry soil. The sound is a gentle hiss, quickly becoming a series of wet plinks as the drops strike the terracotta edge.
The soil darkens immediately where water hits, a spreading stain of moist darkness. The fissures close as the earth swells. Tiny air bubbles rise and pop at the surface, releasing the scent of damp humus—an earthy, slightly sweet smell that fills the air around me. I move the spout in a slow, steady arc, tracing a circle around the stem. The water pools for a second on the surface, then drains downward, pulled by gravity and capillary action. The pot's weight increases perceptibly as the soil saturates.
After about fifteen seconds, water begins to seep from the drainage hole at the bottom, a thin trickle that runs across the saucer. I stop pouring. The final drops detach from the rose and fall in slow succession. I lift the spout and tip it upward to catch any remaining drips. The watering can is now noticeably lighter, its centre of mass shifted backward.
I move to the next plant—a fern with feathery fronds in a glazed ceramic pot. Its soil is less dry, still faintly cool to the touch. I tilt the can again, but at a steeper angle to avoid wetting the delicate leaves. The water stream is narrower, faster, hitting the soil with more force before the rose diffuses it. A few stray droplets land on the fronds, beading up like tiny lenses. The beads catch the light, momentarily magnifying the green tissue beneath. I wipe them away with a fingertip, leaving a faint smear of dust and moisture.
As I work through the collection—a succulent, a spider plant, a struggling orchid—the watering can's weight decreases steadily. Each pour requires a finer adjustment of wrist angle and pour speed. The succulent's gravelly mix absorbs water almost instantly, the fine pebbles clinking as they settle. The spider plant's dense root mass sits tightly in its pot; water pools on top for a full second before vanishing down hidden channels. The orchid, planted in bark chips, releases a sharp, clean scent of wet wood.
When the can is empty, my right arm feels a slight fatigue in the shoulder and forearm. I set the can down on the floor with a dull clank. My hands are dry, though a few droplets cling to the backs of my fingers. I survey the plants: each pot now has a damp, darkened soil surface; a few saucers hold shallow puddles of amber‑tinged water. The leaves, even the drooping ones, have lost nothing visible, but I know that within the hour they will regain their stiffness as water fills the cells, restoring turgor pressure. The air in the room is heavier now, humid with the faint, mingled perfume of wet earth and living green.
That is the memory: the weight, the pour, the darkening soil, the scent, the steady lightening of the can. Nothing more.

Example 6: Separating frozen burger patties
I have pried apart frozen burger patties so many times that the memory is no longer of any single pair, any single knife, any single hand. What remains is a sequence of physical events.
First, the chill that seeps through my fingers even before I touch them. Two palm‑sized discs, stacked, their edges fused by a crust of frost. The top one sits slightly askew, a millimeter of frozen fat bridging the gap. They rest on a cutting board, the wood grain faintly visible beneath a dusting of ice crystals. My left hand, dry and warm, steadies the stack; the right one reaches for the knife.
The blade is thin, its edge honed to a clean line of light. I bring the tip to the seam where the two patties meet, a hairline crack filled with milky ice. The angle is shallow, perhaps fifteen degrees from horizontal. I press forward with a force just shy of cutting—more a persuasive nudge. The metal slides in, a cold‑on‑cold whisper, the sound of crystal boundaries resisting then yielding. The blade settles a quarter‑inch deep, wedged between frozen surfaces.
Now the prying. I rotate the knife handle downward, slow and steady, using the fulcrum of the cutting board. The lower patty stays put, anchored by its own weight and the grit of frost against wood. The upper patty begins to lift, a crack propagating through the ice film with a series of tiny, sharp pops—like thin glass breaking. A puff of frost‑dust rises where the seal parts. The patty tilts, its exposed underside a study in pink‑brown marbled fat, glazed with a thin layer that instantly starts to sweat in the kitchen air.
I stop when the angle reaches about thirty degrees. The knife withdraws, and I set it aside. My right thumb and forefinger grip the lifted edge of the upper patty. The frozen surface bites, a dry, adhesive cold that numbs in seconds. I peel it back the rest of the way, a gentle, tensile pull. The last connection breaks with a wet snap—some spot had already begun to thaw from the heat of my earlier touch. The upper patty comes away whole, its underside now patterned with a faint relief of the lower one's surface, a mirror in frozen meat.
The lower patty lies on the board, a few stray ice chips scattered around it like broken glass. A thin film of moisture shines where the blade had been. The separated patties are no longer a block but two distinct objects, each with its own topography of grind lines and fat speckles. The frost around the edges is already melting into tiny beads of water that catch the light.
I do not cook them. I hold the upper patty for a moment, feeling the cold retreat from my fingers, then place it beside its former companion. The knife carries a smear of frozen fat along its spine. That is the memory: the insertion, the leverage, the fracture, the separation—nothing more, nothing less.
