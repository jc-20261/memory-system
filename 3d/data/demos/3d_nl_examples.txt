I take the apple from the bowl — a Honeycrisp, cold from the refrigerator, its skin tight and waxy under my thumb. It's heavier than it looks, dense with water. I turn it under the tap, run my palm over the whole surface, feeling the shallow dimple where the stem sits in its small crater, the faint raised line of a scar where a branch rubbed it months ago. The water beads and rolls off in the same instant. I dry it on my shirt, and the skin goes from glossy to a dull, matte red.

I set it on the cutting board, stem-side up, and press down. It doesn't roll. The knife is a paring knife, short-bladed, the handle worn pale at the rivets. I hold it in my right hand, thumb braced against the bolster. My left hand pins the apple, fingertips curled back.

First cut: I sink the blade just beside the stem, maybe a quarter inch deep, until I feel the resistance change — the slight pop as the edge breaks through the skin into the flesh. The skin parts along a clean line, pale yellow-white beneath, weeping a thin film of juice immediately. I begin to rotate the apple against the blade, my left thumb pushing the fruit forward in small increments, the knife staying nearly still. The peel comes off in a continuous ribbon, widening to about an inch, curling away from the fruit and dropping in a loose spiral onto the board.

Where the peel separates, the flesh is wet and faintly green-white, and after about ten seconds it starts to dull toward tan. The smell rises — green, sharp, faintly floral. The blade makes a soft, wet, rasping sound, like paper tearing slowly. At the bottom, the peel narrows and I have to lift the knife and reposition it twice to get around the calyx end, where the skin puckers into five small points.

Now the apple is naked, slightly slippery. I turn it in my hand. There's a shallow gouge where I pressed too hard near the stem. Juice runs down my wrist to the heel of my palm and dries tacky.

I quarter it: blade down through the core, a firm crunch, then a second cut, and the four pieces fall apart. I lay each quarter flat and slice out the core — a triangular wedge of fibrous, pale material, slightly translucent, containing three dark brown seeds, pointed at both ends, each in its own papery pocket. I flick the seeds into the sink; they click against the steel.

The slices go into a bowl. The peels go into the compost crock, already browning at the edges. My hands smell like apple for the rest of the morning, even after I wash them.
